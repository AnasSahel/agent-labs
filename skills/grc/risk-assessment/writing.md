# Writing

How every GRC skill writes text a person will read or send under their own name: a report's prose, an executive summary, a list of fixes, an email, a slide. Two leading words: the text is **plain**, and every fix is a **measure**. Tables of ids, the register and the traces are not concerned.

## Plain

Plain means simple, correct and professional. It reads as the user wrote it on a normal working day.

- **Simple is not familiar.** Short sentences and everyday words, but no slang and no spoken shortcuts (in French: `cela`, not `ça`).
- **Their words.** Use the terms of the user, of the documents and of the organisation, as they are, even when another word looks more correct. A term the user corrects is corrected everywhere, in every version of the text.
- **No crafted phrasing.** None of these, in any language:
  - the "not X, but Y" turn: say Y;
  - a short punchy line that closes a paragraph;
  - a sentence that announces an angle before the content;
  - a heading that only labels the reasoning (`Why`, `The rationale`): the content goes right under the position;
  - every line in the same `Label: sentence` mould.
- **Labels and lists.** When items are compared or numbered (options, questions, measures), each starts with its label in bold (`**Option 1**: ...`). Several items are a list, one per line, never one long sentence.
- **Few possessives.** `the server`, `the mailbox`, not `our server`, `our mailbox` on every line.
- **A term the reader may not know** is said in plain words first, or left out.
- **Position first.** An opinion opens with what is recommended, in one sentence (`I recommend option B`), not with a verdict label.
- **The user's own text.** When the user sends their version, fix what is wrong (facts, spelling, grammar) and list what you changed. Their sentences stay.
- **Shorter, nothing lost.** Asked to shorten, remove what is said twice and keep every fact, measure and name. When that is not enough, list what would go and ask before removing it.

## Measures

A **measure** is one thing to do that lowers the risk: a control in a treatment, a fix for a gap, a condition on a vendor.

- **A verb and a thing.** It starts with a verb and names what is done (`Turn on two-step login for the admin accounts`), never an intention (`Strengthen authentication`). A measure that asks for a document says what goes in it.
- **One list, by priority.** The measure that lowers the risk most comes first, and one line says the list is in priority order. No split between "before go-live" and "later", and no measure made a condition of a launch: that is the owner's decision. Ask it when it matters, never assume it.
- **Who does it is a fact or a question.** A team, a role or a person comes from the documents or the user. Unknown: the measure is to decide it (`Define who handles it and write it down`), or the owner is `to name`. Never a role you find likely (the CISO, legal).
- **Its state is a fact too.** Not known to be in place: `If not already in place, ...`. Said to be planned: written as planned, with its date asked.
- **Within the product's limits.** What a product can and cannot do comes from the vendor's documentation or reply, or from the user, with its source. Not checked: say so and write it as a point to check. The measures work around the limits, they do not wish them away.
- **Proportionate.** The lightest check that proves the point. A measure that protects someone other than the organisation, or that the user sets aside, is left out, not argued again.
- **No reference in a message.** Control ids and clauses stay in the report tables. An email or a slide to colleagues carries none, unless the user asks.

## Check what you show

Look at every slide or page before you hand it over (open or screenshot it): text that overlaps or runs off is fixed first. When you cannot look, say so with the file.
